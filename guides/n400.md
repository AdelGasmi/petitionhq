# Drafting Guide — N-400 Application for Naturalization

The N-400 is the application to become a US citizen. The form itself requires no mandatory letters. Letters arise when USCIS has a specific concern about the applicant's eligibility — particularly good moral character (GMC) — or when the applicant wants to proactively address a known issue.

Governing law: INA § 316 (continuous residence), INA § 316(a)(3) (good moral character), 8 CFR § 316. The GMC period is 5 years before filing (or 3 years for spousal petitions), but USCIS can look outside this window for particularly serious conduct.

**Key drafting principles (apply across all N-400 letters):**
- **Specificity over sincerity**: "Has volunteered every Saturday at the St. Vincent de Paul food bank for four years" beats "is generous with his time." Officers discount praise without evidence.
- **Acknowledge the hard fact first**: If there is an arrest, citation, or incident, name it in the letter rather than burying it. Officers know from the background check. Letters that sidestep the known record lose credibility.
- **Rehabilitation arc**: Incident → immediate response → sustained change → present standing. Each stage needs a concrete data point, not a sentiment.
- **Voice consistency**: A corporate employer writes differently than a parish priest. The AI should calibrate tone to the affiant's described background. Do not use legal language in character letters — if USCIS can tell the attorney drafted it, it is discounted.
- **No duplicate phrasing**: When multiple character letters are submitted, they must not share template language. Each letter must originate from the affiant's distinct perspective and vocabulary.

## character-reference — Good Moral Character Reference Letter

### When This Letter Is Used

Character reference letters are submitted when:
1. The applicant has an arrest, citation, or charge on their record — even if dismissed, expunged, or resulted in no conviction
2. The applicant has tax issues, child support delinquency, or other civil matters USCIS flags as GMC-relevant
3. The applicant wants to proactively strengthen a borderline GMC record
4. USCIS issues an RFE or NOID specifically citing GMC concerns

They are NOT routine submissions — a strong GMC record with no issues does not need character letters. Submit them strategically when there is something to address or strengthen.

### What GMC Means in This Context

USCIS uses the statutory framework at INA § 101(f) — bars to GMC — and evaluates the applicant's overall conduct. Adjudicators look at:
- Criminal history (including arrests without conviction, which must be disclosed)
- Tax compliance
- Child/spousal support obligations
- Truthfulness on government forms
- Community standing

A character reference does not expunge a record. It provides human context that helps an adjudicator exercise discretion favorably. The most effective letters come from people who (a) know the applicant well over time, (b) are aware of the relevant incident, and (c) can speak to the applicant's rehabilitation or character despite it.

### Required Elements (write in this order)

**Para 1 — Affiant identity and authority**
Who is this person? Name, occupation, city/state. How long have they known the applicant and in what capacity? Why should USCIS care what they think? Specificity matters: a longtime employer carries more weight than a recent acquaintance. A community leader, religious figure, or civic official carries institutional credibility.

Example: "My name is Patricia Hwang. I am the Director of Community Programs at the Austin Immigrant Rights Coalition, where I have worked for 12 years. I have known [Applicant Name] for seven years, initially through their volunteer work with our organization and subsequently as a personal friend."

**Para 2 — The applicant's character in concrete terms**
Specific, factual, observed behavior. What has the affiant seen firsthand? Employment history, community involvement, family responsibilities, volunteer work, civic participation. Avoid generic praise. Quantify where possible: "has volunteered every Saturday morning at the St. Vincent de Paul food bank for four years" is better than "is generous with his time."

**Para 3 — Addressing the specific GMC concern (if one exists)**
If the affiant is aware of the arrest, incident, or record that triggered the letter — say so. Then say what they know of the circumstances and the applicant's response. Do not minimize or deny; contextualize. "I am aware that [Applicant] was arrested in 2019 following a dispute with a neighbor. I was in contact with [Applicant] throughout that period. What I observed was someone who took immediate responsibility, completed the required anger management program voluntarily before it was court-ordered, and has had no further incidents in the five years since."

If the affiant is not aware of any specific incident, do not mention one. This para is only for letters addressing a known concern.

**Para 4 — Assessment of US commitment and citizenship readiness**
Has the affiant observed the applicant's integration into US life? Community ties, civic participation, family roots, employment stability, language proficiency? Keep this factual and observed — not speculative about what the applicant "will" do.

**Closing**
"I recommend [Applicant Name] without reservation for US citizenship. I am available to answer any questions at [phone/email]." Signed and dated. No notarization required for character letters (unlike sworn affidavits).

### Tone and Voice

This must sound like a real person speaking from genuine knowledge. Not a form letter. The affiant's voice should be consistent with their described background — a community organizer writes differently than a corporate employer.

USCIS officers read dozens of these per week. The ones that stand out:
- Open with a specific, memorable observation rather than a generic qualifier
- Acknowledge difficult facts directly rather than avoiding them
- End with a statement of personal willingness to be contacted

The ones that are discounted:
- "John is a wonderful person and a great friend"
- Generic praise with no specifics
- No acknowledgment of the relevant concern
- Identical phrasing to another letter in the package (suggests they were all drafted by one person)

### Specificity Formula

For each character claim, apply: **[Name] + [date/timeframe] + [specific place or context] + [observed behavior]**

Weak: "He is responsible with money."
Strong: "When Jamal lost his job in March 2022, he came to me to restructure a small personal loan I had made him years earlier. He insisted on continuing monthly payments even when I offered to pause them, and he paid off the balance in full by November 2022."

### Rehabilitation Arc Template (for letters addressing a known incident)

1. **Incident acknowledgment**: "I am aware that [Name] was [arrested/charged/cited] in [Year] for [specific allegation]."
2. **Affiant's observation at the time**: What did the affiant observe or hear from the applicant during that period?
3. **Immediate response**: What did the applicant do right after? (Sought counsel, enrolled in counseling, completed community service, paid restitution.)
4. **Sustained change**: What has the affiant observed in the period since that demonstrates the incident was not representative of character?
5. **Present standing**: Where is the applicant now? Employment, family, community role.

### Format

- Length: 400–700 words
- On the affiant's professional letterhead if they have one, or plain paper
- Signed with typed name, title, and contact info
- Date spelled out
- No notarization required (unlike affidavits)

## discretion-statement — Applicant Discretion Statement / Explanation Letter

### When This Letter Is Used

This is the applicant's own letter explaining a specific circumstance that could affect their naturalization eligibility:
- Arrests or charges (must be disclosed regardless of outcome)
- Extended trips abroad (continuous residence may be broken by trips over 6 months)
- Failure to file or pay taxes
- Failure to register for Selective Service (for male applicants who were 18–26 and required to register)
- Prior claims to US citizenship
- Any "Yes" answer on the N-400 that requires explanation

This is NOT optional if the applicant has a "Yes" answer to a relevant N-400 question. USCIS expects an explanation letter.

### Required Structure

**Header**: Date. Addressed to USCIS. Re: line with applicant's full name and A Number.

**Opening**: State the purpose. "I, [Full Name], submit this statement to explain [the circumstances of / my answer to Question X on Form N-400]."

**Factual account** (the body):
State the facts clearly and chronologically. For an arrest: when did it happen, what was alleged, what was the outcome (dismissed, no charges filed, guilty plea, conviction), what was the sentence if any, and what has happened since. Be accurate — USCIS will have access to background check results. Inconsistency between the statement and the record is a serious problem.

Do NOT minimize or use euphemism. "I was arrested" not "there was an incident." "I pleaded guilty to a misdemeanor charge of disorderly conduct" not "I had a minor brush with the law."

For extended trips abroad: state exact dates of departure and return, purpose of the trip, and any documentation that shows ties to the US were maintained (continued to pay rent or mortgage, paid US taxes, maintained US employment, kept US bank accounts). If the trip was for a qualifying reason (medical emergency of a family member, overseas employment with a US company), state that and reference the supporting documents.

For Selective Service: if the applicant failed to register, explain why (was not aware of the requirement, was not living in the US during the registration window, etc.) and note whether USCIS has previously found the applicant's failure to register was not knowing and willful. Reference any Selective Service documentation.

**Rehabilitation or context** (where applicable):
After the factual account: has the applicant completed any required conditions (probation, community service, counseling)? Have they had any further incidents? What has changed in their life since?

**Closing declaration**:
"I declare under penalty of perjury that the foregoing is true and correct to the best of my knowledge and belief." Signed by the applicant (not the attorney).

### Critical Rules

- **Applicant must sign** — not the attorney, not a family member
- **Match the N-400 answers exactly** — if the form says "Yes" to Question 23, the letter must address what Question 23 asks, using the same terminology
- **Accuracy over self-protection** — USCIS will compare this statement against the background check. Any inconsistency — even innocent omission — harms credibility
- **No legal argument** — this is a factual statement, not a brief. Legal argument belongs in an attorney's cover letter if applicable
- Do NOT notarize unless specifically requested by USCIS

### Language Template — Extended Absence Explanation

Use when the applicant had one or more trips abroad exceeding 6 months (continuous residence risk):

"From [departure date] to [return date], I was present in [Country] for a period of [X months]. I traveled for the following reason: [specific reason — medical care for a parent, overseas posting by US employer, family emergency]. During this period, I maintained my ties to the United States as follows: I continued to pay rent on my apartment at [address]; I continued to file US federal and state income taxes (returns attached); I maintained my US bank accounts and credit cards; and I continued to be employed by [Employer], working remotely under written authorization. I did not intend to abandon my residence in the United States and returned as soon as [the medical situation resolved / my assignment concluded / etc.]."

Attach: IRS transcripts for the years of the extended absence; lease or mortgage statements; employment authorization or foreign posting letter if applicable.
