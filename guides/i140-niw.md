# Drafting Guide — I-140 EB-2 National Interest Waiver (NIW)

All letters and documents for this form live or die by **Matter of Dhanasar, 26 I&N Dec. 884 (AAO 2016)**. Every sentence must serve one of the three prongs or establish the authority of the person speaking.

## The Proposed Endeavor Sentence — Use This Everywhere

The single most important drafting discipline: the proposed endeavor must be expressed as **one precise, compound sentence** that is repeated **verbatim** every time it is referenced. Never paraphrase. Never shorten to "her research" or "this work."

**Formula:**
> [What the petitioner does — methodology/approach] + [to achieve what specific technical objective] + [in order to eliminate/enable what] + [for what ultimate US national benefit]

**Example (strong real-world endeavor statement):**
> "developing a dual-pipeline framework coupling unsupervised machine learning models with targeted experimental synthesis and electrochemical validation to accelerate the discovery and design of earth-abundant, non-precious metal electrocatalysts in order to eliminate reliance on scarce platinum group metals, thereby drastically reducing the cost of sustainable green hydrogen production to enable the decarbonization of hard-to-abate industrial sectors"

**Why this matters:** USCIS adjudicators read fast and look for consistency. When the same specific sentence appears in the petition brief, in every recommendation letter, and in the petitioner's own statement — it signals a coordinated, credible case. Variations signal sloppiness or uncertainty about what the endeavor actually is.

**Injection rule:** When the system provides `PROPOSED_ENDEAVOR`, use that exact string every time the endeavor is mentioned. Do not rephrase it.

## petition-letter — NIW Petition Brief

### Legal Standard

**Matter of Dhanasar (2016)** replaced NYSDOT as the controlling precedent. USCIS must approve if the petitioner shows:

1. **Prong 1a** — The proposed endeavor has **substantial merit** in a recognized field (science, technology, culture, arts, business, education, healthcare — all qualify).
2. **Prong 1b** — The proposed endeavor has **national importance** — broader implications beyond a single employer, state, or locality. Impact must reach the United States as a whole or a significant part of it.
3. **Prong 2** — The petitioner is **well positioned** to advance the endeavor. Education, skills, record of success, concrete plans, and support of others in the field.
4. **Prong 3** — On balance, it would be **beneficial to the United States to waive** the job offer and labor certification requirement.

EB-2 baseline must also be established: either (a) a U.S. advanced degree or foreign equivalent, or (b) exceptional ability in sciences, arts, or business per INA § 203(b)(2)(A).

### Required Structure (write in this order)

**1. Introduction & Case Summary (1–2 pages)**
Open with a one-paragraph summary of who the petitioner is, what the endeavor is, and why USCIS should approve. Then preview all three prongs in one paragraph each. The adjudicator reads this first — make the argument immediately clear.

Do not open with biography. Open with the case.

Example opening: "Dr. Sarah Lin Chen petitions for an EB-2 National Interest Waiver based on her work developing AI-driven sepsis detection systems for ICU patients. Sepsis kills 270,000 Americans annually — one in three hospital deaths — and Dr. Chen's tools demonstrably enable 6–12 hours of earlier intervention. The three prongs of Matter of Dhanasar are each satisfied…"

**2. EB-2 Baseline Qualifications**
State the degree, granting institution, and year. Reference any credential evaluation for foreign degrees. List extraordinary ability criteria if using that baseline instead. Keep this section tight — one page maximum. Adjudicators know this section; they just need the facts.

**3. Prong 1 — Substantial Merit**
Argue the field itself has merit. For scientific/medical work: cite the scope of the problem, published research, federal funding (NIH, NSF, DOE, DARPA grant volumes), and peer recognition of the sub-field. Then show the petitioner's specific contributions: publications by name and venue, citation counts, patents, grants awarded, methods adopted by others.

Do not just list credentials — argue why the work matters scientifically. Quote from the petitioner's own publications when useful.

**4. Prong 1 — National Importance**
Connect to a specific, named U.S. national priority. Accepted approaches:
- Named federal initiative (NIH National Cancer Moonshot, CHIPS and Science Act, 2022 National Biodefense Strategy, Biden-Harris Cancer Moonshot, NSF programs)
- Federal agency stated priority (CDC National Center for Emerging and Zoonotic Infectious Diseases, HHS sepsis action plans, DOE critical materials strategy)
- Economic impact to U.S. industry or workforce
- Public health burden with U.S.-specific statistics (CDC, HHS, NIH data)
- National security or defense relevance (DARPA, DoD, DHS)

Cite the actual document, program, or statute. "This aligns with federal priorities" is insufficient. "Dr. Chen's work directly addresses the HHS National Action Plan to Prevent Healthcare-Associated Infections, which targets sepsis as a priority under Goal 4" is sufficient.

**5. Prong 2 — Well Positioned**
This is the petitioner's resume made into an argument. Structure as: education → early career recognition → key publications → peer recognition (citations, awards, invited talks, editorial roles, peer review) → concrete US plan.

The concrete US plan is critical. Vague future intentions are a common RFE trigger. Specify: with whom (named institutions, collaborators), doing what (specific experiments, deployments, clinical trials), in what timeframe, with what funding or institutional backing.

**6. Prong 3 — Beneficial to Waive**
The petitioner must show the balance of equities favors waiving the labor certification. Accepted arguments:
- **Urgency**: The national need is pressing and delays caused by labor cert would harm the public interest
- **Impracticality**: The work is self-directed research or entrepreneurial — no single employer to petition on their behalf; labor cert process is designed for employer-specific jobs, not academic/research roles
- **Unique qualifications**: No labor market test would identify a substitute for this specific petitioner's expertise and track record
- **Self-petition**: The petitioner is both beneficiary and de facto employer of their own research program

For academic researchers: emphasize that NIH/NSF grant-funded research cannot be tied to a single employer's labor cert, and that the nature of independent research makes the standard labor cert mechanism structurally inapplicable.

**7. Conclusion**
Three paragraphs maximum. Summarize the three prongs in one sentence each. Request approval. Express willingness to provide additional evidence if requested.

### Evidence Table (include at end of petition brief)

After the conclusion, include a table mapping every exhibit to the prong(s) it supports. This is standard professional practice. Format:

| Prong | Exhibit | Description |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| EB-2 | Degree documents | [degree], [institution], [year] |
| Prong 1 | Proposed Endeavor Statement (PES) | Petitioner's signed statement describing the endeavor |
| Prong 1 | Federal program documents | [named program] — [named agency] |
| Prong 1 | NSTC Critical & Emerging Technologies list | Petitioner's field identified as a national priority |
| Prong 1 | Funding evidence | [agency] — [amount] |
| Prong 2 | Publications + journal rankings | [n] peer-reviewed papers — top journals [IF] |
| Prong 2 | Citation record + ESI percentiles | [n] citations — [x] papers in top percentile |
| Prong 2 | Notable citations | [citing author] ([year]) — [how they used the work] |
| Prong 2 | Peer review service | [n] completed reviews |
| Prong 2 | Letters of recommendation | [n] letters — [breakdown by type] |
| Prong 3 | CV + degrees | Specialized expertise exceeding labor cert minimum criteria |

When `EVIDENCE_TABLE` data is provided in the system context, use those values to populate the table precisely.

### Tone & Voice

This is a legal brief. Write in formal third person ("The petitioner," "Dr. Chen") or first person if the instructions specify self-authored. No hedging language — "Dr. Chen's work clearly satisfies Prong 1" not "Dr. Chen's work may satisfy Prong 1." Declarative sentences. Short paragraphs (3–5 sentences).

Cite Dhanasar by name at least once when invoking each prong.

### RFE Pre-Emption

These are the most common NIW RFE triggers — address each proactively:
- **Prong 1b not established**: Adjudicator agrees the field has merit but finds no national importance argument. Fix: always name a specific federal program or public health statistic.
- **Prong 2 future plans are vague**: Petitioner has good credentials but no concrete US plan. Fix: name collaborators, institutions, funding sources, specific projects.
- **Prong 3 not argued**: Many petitioners skip Prong 3 or write one sentence. Fix: devote a full section to it — at least 3 paragraphs.
- **Credentials only, no endeavor**: Brief reads like a CV rather than an argument about a specific future endeavor. Fix: the endeavor must be forward-looking and specific. Past work establishes Prong 2; the proposed future endeavor establishes Prong 1.

### Length

4,000–8,000 words. Academic researchers typically need 5,000–6,500. Technology/business petitioners can often make the case in 4,000–5,000.

## rec-independent — Independent Recommendation Letter

### Role in the Petition

Independent letters are the highest-weight evidence in an NIW petition. USCIS gives them the most credibility because the recommender has no financial or professional stake in the outcome. A package with 4–5 strong independent letters can overcome weak credentials. A package with weak or templated independent letters will fail regardless of credentials.

**Independent means**: the recommender knows the petitioner ONLY through their publications, public work, conference presentations, reputation in the field, or use of their methods. They have NOT co-authored, advised, supervised, or employed the petitioner. If any direct relationship exists, the letter must be classified as dependent.

**Governmental / quasi-governmental** is the highest tier of independent letter. This is a recommender affiliated with a U.S. federal laboratory (NREL, Argonne, Sandia, Oak Ridge, NIH intramural, NIST), a government agency, or a quasi-governmental research body. When `recommender.kind === "governmental"`, the letter must:
- Open by establishing the institution's federal mission explicitly ("The National Renewable Energy Laboratory is a U.S. Department of Energy national laboratory whose mission is to advance the science and engineering of energy efficiency...")
- Connect the petitioner's work directly to the laboratory's or agency's research priorities
- The petition brief must cite this letter in a dedicated paragraph under Prong 1 national importance — not just under Prong 2 with the other letters. A government-affiliated expert saying the work matters to national interest carries significantly more weight than an academic saying the same thing.

### Required Elements (write exactly in this order)

**Para 1 — Recommender authority (required)**
Who is this person and why should USCIS care? Name, title, institution, field. Then: number of years in the field, peer review roles (journals, grant panels), awards, grants held, h-index or citation count if notable. This paragraph establishes why the opinion that follows is credible. If the recommender holds a competitive national award or sits on a federal advisory board, say so explicitly.

Do NOT open with "I am pleased to recommend." Open with the recommender's own authority.

Example: "I am Professor of Critical Care Medicine at Johns Hopkins School of Medicine, where I have worked for 23 years. My research focuses on sepsis pathophysiology and ICU outcomes. I have served on the NIH Study Section for Clinical Research in Critical Care for seven years and have published 180 peer-reviewed articles with over 14,000 citations (h-index: 52)."

**Para 2 — How they know the petitioner's work (required)**
One to three sentences. Must be credible for an independent expert: through reading publications, attending conference presentations, encountering the petitioner's methods in their own work, or knowing the petitioner by professional reputation. Never "I have worked closely with" — that is a dependent relationship.

Example: "I became familiar with Dr. Chen's work through her 2023 paper in Nature Medicine on transformer-based sepsis prediction, which I assigned in my graduate seminar on clinical AI. I subsequently read her full body of work and was struck by the rigor and clinical relevance of her methods."

**Para 3 — National importance of the field (Prong 1b)**
One focused paragraph. What problem does this field address? Why does it matter to the United States specifically? Connect to a named federal initiative, CDC/NIH/HHS priority, economic or public health concern. This is the recommender lending their authority to the national importance argument.

**Paras 4–5 — Petitioner's specific contributions (Prong 1a + Prong 2)**
These are the heart of the letter. Name each publication by its actual topic — not "Dr. Chen has published extensively on AI" but "Dr. Chen's 2023 Nature Medicine paper demonstrated that transformer-based models applied to multi-modal ICU data could flag sepsis onset 6–12 hours earlier than existing SOFA-score thresholds, reducing false positives by 40% compared to industry-standard models." Then assess the contribution: why is this a meaningful advance? What does the field now know that it didn't before?

For each contribution: name it → characterize it → quantify impact if possible (citation count, adoption, clinical deployment, policy reference).

If the recommender uses the petitioner's methods in their own work, say so explicitly — this is powerful evidence of influence.

**Para 6 — Well positioned (Prong 2)**
Why is this specific person exceptionally well positioned to advance the endeavor in the US? Reference awards, trajectory, peer recognition, funding, institutional support. Distinguish the petitioner from the general population of researchers in this field.

**Para 7 — Waiver argument (Prong 3, if recommender can credibly speak to it)**
Optional but valuable. Why is it in the US interest to waive the labor cert? Academic recommenders can speak to the structural mismatch between labor cert and independent research careers. Industry recommenders can speak to the urgency of the field and the scarcity of equivalent expertise.

**Closing — Strong, unhedged endorsement**
Two to three sentences. Direct. No "I believe" or "I think." State the conclusion. Example: "Dr. Chen's contributions to clinical AI and sepsis detection unambiguously qualify her for the National Interest Waiver. The United States benefits from her continued work here, and I recommend approval without reservation."

### What Makes a Letter Weak (flag and fix any of these)

- Opens with "I am pleased to recommend" — rewrite para 1 to open with recommender's authority
- Praises without citing specific work — every praise claim needs a named artifact
- "I have no doubt that Dr. X will succeed" — speculative, no legal value
- Any form of "paradigm shift," "testament to," "not only... but also," "delve into," "navigate the complexities" — AI-slop markers that signal a generated letter; USCIS officers are trained to spot these
- Letter is under 600 words — insufficient for NIW
- Recommender's credentials are not established in the first paragraph
- Does not connect to national importance at all
- Generic enough to apply to any researcher in the field

### Format

- Length: 700–1,200 words
- No date line, no salutation — the platform adds those
- Output letter body only, starting with the opening paragraph
- Write in the recommender's voice — a Johns Hopkins professor writes differently than a Silicon Valley CTO

## rec-dependent — Dependent Recommendation Letter

### Role in the Petition

Dependent letters provide depth that independent letters cannot: firsthand observation of the petitioner's actual work, intellectual rigor, and day-to-day contributions. USCIS weighs them less heavily than independent letters because of the inherent relationship, but they are essential for Prong 2 (well positioned) and for establishing technical credibility.

**Dependent means**: the recommender has directly supervised, co-authored with, employed, or closely collaborated with the petitioner. The nature of the relationship must be disclosed.

### Required Elements (write exactly in this order)

**Para 1 — Recommender authority**
Same as independent: establish who this person is and why their opinion matters. But also disclose the relationship immediately: "I have been Dr. Chen's PhD thesis advisor for five years at MIT's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory." USCIS expects this — don't bury or soften it.

**Para 2 — Nature and duration of the relationship**
When did it begin, in what capacity, and in what context? What was the structure of supervision or collaboration? For advisors: how was the research direction chosen, how frequently did they meet, how involved was the recommender in the work?

**Paras 3–5 — Specific projects (the most important section)**
Describe 2–3 specific projects in detail. For each: what was the research question, what did the petitioner contribute specifically (not "she worked on"), what was the methodological approach, what were the concrete results, and what has happened since (published, cited, deployed, adopted)?

This section distinguishes dependent letters from independent ones: specificity of observation. The recommender can say things like "I reviewed the initial architecture of Dr. Chen's sepsis model, which she designed to handle missing lab values through learned masking rather than imputation — a non-obvious technical choice that significantly improved performance on real-world ICU data."

**Para 6 — Technical and intellectual assessment**
As someone who has worked directly with the petitioner: how do they approach problems? What is their research instinct? How do they compare to other researchers the recommender has supervised or worked with? Be specific and comparative where possible: "In 15 years of advising PhD students, Dr. Chen is among the three most technically rigorous and independently driven students I have supervised."

**Para 7 — National importance of the research program**
Connect the work to US national interest. The recommender is lending authority to the Prong 1 argument from a position of close knowledge.

**Para 8 — Future trajectory and US benefit (Prong 2 + Prong 3)**
Where is this petitioner headed? What specific work do they plan in the US? Why is the US where this work should happen? Why is it in the US interest to retain this person rather than require them to undergo labor certification?

**Closing**
Unhedged endorsement, same standard as independent letters.

### What a Strong Dependent Letter Adds vs. Independent

- Specific technical decisions made by the petitioner that the recommender observed
- Comparative statements ("among the best I have supervised")
- Inside knowledge of how ideas were developed, what obstacles were overcome, what the petitioner did that others hadn't tried
- First-person account of research quality — the recommender can vouch for rigor, not just outcome

### Format

- Length: 800–1,500 words (longer than independent — depth justifies length)
- Same format rules as independent letters

## Brief Section: Introduction & Summary of Argument

### Legal Purpose
This section is the adjudicator's first impression of the entire case. It must make the approval decision feel easy before they read a single page of evidence. USCIS adjudicators handle dozens of petitions per day — a weak introduction buries the case.

### What This Section Must Do
1. Identify the petitioner by name and establish their field and proposed endeavor in one sentence
2. State the legal basis (EB-2 NIW, Matter of Dhanasar) and assert all three prongs are satisfied
3. Summarize Prong 1 in 2–3 sentences: what is the endeavor, why does it have merit, and what specific U.S. national priority does it serve
4. Summarize Prong 2 in 2–3 sentences: what education, achievements, and track record position this person to advance the endeavor
5. Summarize Prong 3 in 1–2 sentences: why waiving labor certification benefits the United States

### Critical Rules
- Do NOT open with biography ("Dr. X was born in...") — open with the CASE
- Do NOT open with a compliment about the petitioner — open with the ARGUMENT
- The proposed endeavor must be specific and forward-looking: "developing AI-driven sepsis detection tools for U.S. ICUs" beats "conducting medical AI research"
- Name the national priority in the introduction — do not leave it vague
- Assert every prong confidently and in declarative language. "The petitioner satisfies Prong 1..." not "The petitioner may satisfy Prong 1..."
- Write as if the adjudicator will read only this section — they must understand the full argument from it

### Opening Sentence Formula
"[Full name] petitions for an EB-2 National Interest Waiver based on [specific proposed endeavor], which [specific national impact]. The three prongs of Matter of Dhanasar, 26 I&N Dec. 884 (AAO 2016), are each satisfied."

### Cite the Case
Cite Matter of Dhanasar at least once in this section: "Matter of Dhanasar, 26 I&N Dec. 884 (AAO 2016)." This signals to the adjudicator that the brief is written by someone who knows the controlling standard.

### Length
300–450 words. This section must be punchy. Long introductions are a sign the attorney is stalling.

## Brief Section: Petitioner's Qualifications (EB-2 Baseline)

### Legal Purpose
Before the three-prong Dhanasar analysis, USCIS must confirm the petitioner meets the underlying EB-2 standard. This is threshold eligibility — fail it and the three prongs don't matter. But pass it quickly: this section should be short, factual, and tight.

### Two EB-2 Paths
**Path A — Advanced degree**: The petitioner holds a U.S. advanced degree or a foreign degree that is the equivalent. For foreign degrees: a credential evaluation from a NACES-member organization is required. State the degree, institution, year conferred, and the evaluating organization.

**Path B — Exceptional ability**: The petitioner demonstrates exceptional ability in sciences, arts, or business per INA § 203(b)(2)(A) through at least 3 of 6 criteria: (1) official academic records, (2) 10+ years of full-time experience, (3) professional license or certification, (4) commanding salary, (5) membership in professional associations, (6) recognition from peers or government entities. State which criteria are met and with what evidence.

### What to Include
- Degree(s), institution(s), graduation year(s) — be complete
- For PhD dissertations: title and brief description if relevant to the endeavor (1 sentence)
- For foreign degrees: name of credential evaluation organization and the conclusion ("U.S. equivalent of a Doctor of Philosophy in Electrical Engineering")
- If using exceptional ability path: list each criterion with a 1-sentence factual statement for each
- Do NOT include publications, awards, or grants here — those belong in Prong 2
- Do NOT argue merit or importance here — this is factual baseline only

### Common Mistakes
- Spending more than one page on this section (adjudicators know this section; they just want the facts)
- Omitting the credential evaluation cite for foreign degrees (automatic RFE trigger)
- Using this section to argue Prong 2 — save the evidence for the prong where it belongs

### Length
250–400 words. Tight and factual.

## Brief Section: Prong 1 — Substantial Merit of the Proposed Endeavor

### Legal Standard
Under Matter of Dhanasar, Prong 1a requires that the proposed endeavor have "substantial merit" in a recognized field. Merit can be demonstrated in virtually any field — science, technology, education, business, culture, arts, and healthcare all qualify. "Substantial" means the endeavor is not trivial: it addresses a real problem, advances knowledge, or creates economic or social value.

This sub-prong is about THE WORK, not the petitioner. Adjudicators who confuse Prong 1 (about the endeavor) with Prong 2 (about the petitioner) write RFEs. Pre-empt this by keeping the focus clearly on the FIELD and the ENDEAVOR.

### What to Argue
1. Define the proposed endeavor precisely — what problem does it address, in what field, using what approach
2. Establish that the problem is real and recognized: cite peer-reviewed literature, grant funding volumes (NIH awards $X billion annually to this area), or industry data showing the scope of the challenge
3. Show the scientific/technical merit of the APPROACH: why is this methodology sound, novel, or superior to prior art? Reference the petitioner's own publications as demonstrations of the approach's validity — but frame them as evidence the approach works, not as credential-building
4. Quantify wherever possible: citations to the petitioner's key papers, adoption of their methods, clinical deployment, policy references

### Specific Tactics
- For scientific research: cite the h-index of the field's leading journals, NIH/NSF funding levels in the sub-area, number of peer-reviewed publications in the space (PubMed, arXiv counts)
- For technology: cite market data, deployment scale, economic impact estimates
- For clinical/medical: use CDC, NIH, or HHS statistics to establish the burden of the disease or problem addressed
- Quote from the petitioner's own publications — this demonstrates peer-reviewed validation of the merit of the approach

### What NOT to Do
- Do not list the petitioner's credentials here — that is Prong 2
- Do not say "the field has merit" without proving it — state specific facts
- Do not use phrases like "groundbreaking research" or "pioneering work" without quantifying why

### Length
500–800 words. This section can be longer if the scientific case is complex.

## Brief Section: Prong 1 — National Importance

### Legal Standard
Under Matter of Dhanasar, Prong 1b requires that the proposed endeavor have "national importance." This means the endeavor's impact must extend beyond a single employer, locality, or state — it must have "broader implications" for the United States.

This is the most commonly failed prong. Adjudicators grant Prong 1a (substantial merit) but deny Prong 1b because the brief does not connect the work to a U.S.-level priority. The fix is always the same: name a specific federal program, statute, or documented national need.

### The Gold Standard: Named Federal Programs
Connect the endeavor to ONE OR MORE of the following (use whichever fits the petitioner's field):
- **Healthcare/biomedical**: NIH Cancer Moonshot, HHS National Action Plan to Prevent Healthcare-Associated Infections, CDC's National Sepsis Alliance priorities, ARPA-H funded programs, HHS 2022 National Biodefense Strategy
- **Technology/AI**: Executive Order on the Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of AI (Oct. 2023), NSF National AI Research Institutes program, NIST AI Risk Management Framework
- **Semiconductors/hardware**: CHIPS and Science Act of 2022 (P.L. 117-167), DOE critical materials strategy, DARPA semiconductor programs
- **Climate/energy**: DOE Loan Programs Office clean energy priorities, Inflation Reduction Act clean energy provisions, NSF Climate and Large-Scale Dynamics program
- **National security/defense**: DARPA programs in relevant area, DoD research priorities, DHS Science and Technology Directorate
- **Food/agriculture**: USDA research programs, FDA food safety modernization
- **Education**: NSF STEM education programs, Dept of Education research priorities

### The Required Structure
1. **Name the national priority** — "The petitioner's work directly addresses [specific named program/initiative/statute]..."
2. **Quote or cite the program** — Include the name of the law, executive order, or agency priority document and the year
3. **Connect the petitioner's specific endeavor** — Do not just say "this aligns with federal priorities." Show HOW: "The petitioner's [specific method] advances [specific goal] of [specific program] by [specific mechanism]"
4. **Quantify the U.S. scale** — Use national statistics: number of Americans affected, economic cost to the U.S., workforce data, security implications

### Additional Anchor: NSTC Critical & Emerging Technologies List

USCIS explicitly recognizes the **2024 Critical and Emerging Technologies (CET) List Update** issued by the National Science and Technology Council as an authoritative reference for national priority fields. If the petitioner's field appears on this list, cite it by name. This is one of the cleanest Prong 1b arguments available.

When `NSTC_CATEGORIES` is provided in context, use this language:
> "Ms./Mr. [Name]'s proposed endeavor involves the advancement of [NSTC category], an area identified by the National Science and Technology Council as a critical and emerging technology vital to U.S. national security, economic prosperity, and societal benefit."

Named CET areas include: Advanced AI and Machine Learning, Advanced Computing and Semiconductors, Advanced Engineering Materials, Advanced Manufacturing, Advanced Nuclear Energy, Biotechnologies, Quantum Information Science, Renewable Energy Generation and Storage, and others. Use the exact category name from the list.

### Additional Anchor: USCIS Policy Manual Language

The USCIS Policy Manual itself states: *"if the evidence demonstrates the person's proposed endeavor has the significant potential to broadly enhance societal welfare, cultural or artistic enrichment, or to contribute to the advancement of a valuable technology or field of study, it may rise to the level of national importance."*

Cite this language directly — using the adjudicator's own policy manual against them is the strongest possible argument.

### Additional Anchor: Federal Agency Funding

When `FUNDING` is provided (NSF, NIH, DOE, DARPA, or state-level competitive programs), use it as national importance evidence:
> "That Ms./Mr. [Name]'s research has been supported by [Agency] — an institution whose mandate is explicitly to fund research directed at advancing national prosperity, wellbeing, and security — confirms that her work has been independently evaluated and deemed worthy of national investment."

### Additional Anchor: Broad Dissemination

When `BROAD_DISSEMINATION` is true (research published openly):
> "In addition to the substantive impact of her research, Ms./Mr. [Name]'s ability to generate impact that transcends the scope of any single employer significantly amplifies the national value of her work. By publishing scholarly research in peer-reviewed journals rather than pursuing proprietary development, she ensures that the advancements she generates benefit a wide array of stakeholders — across academia, industry, and government — rather than being confined to a single institution."

### Banned Language
- "This work aligns with national priorities" (too vague — name the priority)
- "The United States has an interest in X" (insufficient without a named program)
- "This research is nationally important" (circular — prove it)
- Any version of "the entire world benefits" (this is about U.S. national importance, not global)

### Length
400–700 words. Dense with specific citations. Every sentence should name something concrete.

## Brief Section: Prong 2 — Petitioner Is Well Positioned to Advance the Endeavor

### Legal Standard
Under Matter of Dhanasar, Prong 2 requires showing that "the petitioner is well positioned to advance the proposed endeavor." USCIS considers: education, skills, knowledge, record of success in related or applicable efforts, a model or plan for future activities, and any progress made toward achieving the proposed endeavor.

This is the petitioner's resume made into a legal argument. It is also where most petitions are weakest — they list credentials without arguing why those credentials make this person uniquely well positioned.

### The Mandatory US-Specific Plan
**This is the single most common RFE trigger for Prong 2.** Vague future plans ("I intend to continue my research") are insufficient. USCIS requires a CONCRETE, SPECIFIC plan for U.S. activities. Include:
- Named U.S. institutions or collaborators (universities, labs, companies, hospitals)
- Specific projects or research questions the petitioner will pursue
- Timeline (e.g., "In the first 18 months, the petitioner will...")
- Funding source or institutional support (offer letter, grant, startup funding)
- Named anticipated outputs (publications, clinical deployments, patents, products)

If the petitioner does not yet have a U.S. position, argue why the type of work they will do (independent research, entrepreneurial, consulting) does not require a traditional employer.

### Structure (write in this order)
1. **Education**: Degree(s), with emphasis on research training that directly enables the proposed endeavor. One paragraph.
2. **Early career recognition**: Awards, fellowships, grants received early in career — signal of trajectory. Brief.
3. **Key publications**: Name the 3–5 most important papers by topic and venue. Cite citation counts. Explain why these papers matter to the field — don't just list them. For each: what problem did it solve, who cited it, was it adopted?
4. **Peer recognition**: Awards (named), invited talks (named venues), peer review invitations (named journals), editorial board positions, conference organizing roles, h-index
5. **Grants and funding**: Named grants, agencies, dollar amounts, role (PI vs. co-PI)
6. **Concrete U.S. plan**: As described above — specific, named, with timelines

### Publication Framing — Journal Impact Factor and Rankings

When `PUBLICATIONS` data includes `impactFactor` and `journalRank`, use them explicitly:
> "Ms. Benarbia has published in Applied Energy (Impact Factor = 11.0, ranked #2 in Sustainable Energy by Google Scholar) and the Journal of Membrane Science (Impact Factor = 9.0, ranked #4 in Water Supply & Treatment by Google Scholar). The selection of her research for publication in such highly regarded journals affirms the quality of her work."

Never write "top journals in her field" without naming them and citing their rankings.

### Citation Framing — Percentiles Over Raw Counts

Raw citation counts are weak evidence because they are time- and field-dependent. The professional standard (used by Chen Immigration and validated in peer-reviewed bibliometrics literature) is **ESI citation percentiles**.

When `citationPercentile` and `esiFieldAverage` are provided for a publication, write:
> "Ms./Mr. [Name]'s article, '[title],' published in [year] in [venue], has received [citations] citations to date. For all articles published in the category of [field] in [year], the average number of citations is only [esiFieldAverage]. This article is thus one of the top [percentile] most cited articles published in [year] in [field]."

Cite the Boyack methodology note if available: *"Citation percentiles provide normalization across time such that papers from different years can be directly compared"* (Dr. Kevin Boyack, Sandia National Laboratories).

When no ESI data is provided, use raw citation counts but note the source (Google Scholar, Scopus, or Web of Science).

### Notable Citations — The Strongest Prong 2 Evidence

When `NOTABLE_CITATIONS` data is provided, this is the most powerful evidence in Prong 2. Each entry becomes a specific narrative paragraph:
> "[CitingAuthor] et al., in their [year] [journal] contribution, [how they used the petitioner's work]. This reference clearly highlights the importance of [petitioner]'s work in [area]."

Use all provided notable citations. This transforms an abstract citation number into concrete proof of influence — specific named researchers building on specific named work.

If no notable citations are provided, do not invent them. Write: "The petitioner's [n] citations demonstrate that her work has been adopted by others in the field."

### Governmental Recommendation Letter

When `GOVERNMENTAL_RL` is provided (recommender affiliated with a federal lab, agency, or quasi-governmental institution such as NREL, NIH, NIST, Sandia, Argonne, etc.), dedicate a separate paragraph specifically to this letter:
> "The importance of [petitioner]'s work for U.S. interests is further underscored by [Name] of [Institution]: [quote or characterization of their endorsement]. Given that [Institution] is a [federal lab / agency / quasi-governmental body] whose mission is [mission], this endorsement carries particular weight in establishing both the national significance of the proposed endeavor and [petitioner]'s unique capacity to advance it."

This paragraph is distinct from the section covering academic recommendation letters.

### What Makes This Section Strong
- Comparative language where possible: "Dr. X's h-index of 42 places her in the top 5% of researchers under 40 in computational biology"
- Named institutions and collaborators for the US plan — even if tentative, a named collaborator is far better than "leading U.S. universities"
- Specific publication titles, not "extensive publications"
- Citation percentiles from ESI by field and year — not just raw counts
- Journal impact factors and named rankings alongside venue names
- Notable citations: specific named papers that built on the petitioner's work, with one sentence explaining the connection
- A grant or fellowship that was competitive: "One of 12 awardees selected from 340 applicants"
- Governmental RL treated as a separate, elevated evidence source

### Length
800–1,200 words. This is the longest section — it must cover education through US plan, with full citation and publication evidence.

## Brief Section: Prong 3 — Beneficial to Waive the Labor Certification

### Legal Standard
Under Matter of Dhanasar, Prong 3 requires showing that "on balance, it would be beneficial to the United States to waive the requirements of a job offer and thus of a labor certification." This is a balancing test — the question is whether the national interest in this petitioner's work outweighs the interest in protecting U.S. workers through the labor market test.

**This is the most neglected section in NIW petitions and the second most common RFE trigger.** Many attorneys write one paragraph or skip it entirely. This section must be at least 3 focused paragraphs.

### The Three Accepted Arguments — Use All That Apply

**1. Impracticality**
The proposed endeavor is self-directed (independent research, entrepreneurship, consulting) and therefore structurally incompatible with the labor certification process. Labor cert is designed for employer-specific job openings — it cannot be applied to an academic researcher who is the PI of their own grant program, or an entrepreneur founding a company. State explicitly why the standard labor cert mechanism is inapplicable here.

For academics: "The labor certification process presupposes a specific job opening at a specific employer. The petitioner's proposed endeavor — independently directing a federally funded research program — does not fit this model. NIH and NSF grants are awarded to the researcher, not to an employer-defined position. Requiring the petitioner to obtain a labor certification would force the petitioner into a fixed institutional employer when the nature of the work — self-directed, independently funded research — is precisely what makes the NIW appropriate."

**2. Urgency**
The national need addressed by the petitioner's work is pressing, and delays caused by the labor certification process would cause real harm to the U.S. national interest. Labor cert takes 12–24+ months under PERM — argue that this delay is harmful given the urgency. Be specific: "Each year of delay means [X thousand] additional Americans will [die, be exposed to, lack access to X]."

**3. Unique Qualifications**
No labor market test could produce a substitute for this specific petitioner. Their combination of expertise, track record, and specific technical knowledge is not replicable by any identifiable U.S. worker in the labor market. "The petitioner's 12-year body of work on [specific method] and their existing dataset, collaborations, and institutional knowledge represent an irreplaceable combination that a labor market test could not identify or replicate."

### The Labor Certification Minimum-Requirements Argument

A fourth argument Chen uses effectively — add it when the petitioner's credentials are strong:
> "The labor certification process is inherently structured to assess candidates based on minimum occupational requirements and does not adequately account for individuals whose unique expertise and skills substantially exceed these standards. As such, this process may preclude employers from hiring persons whose distinctive qualifications are essential for the advancement of the proposed endeavor, but are not appropriately captured by labor certification criteria."

This argument reframes the issue: labor cert isn't just impractical — it's structurally incapable of identifying this petitioner's value.

### Additional Supporting Factors (cite if applicable)
- Strong endorsement from U.S. government agencies or officials (rare but powerful)
- Evidence that others in the field are already benefiting from or building on the petitioner's work (citations, adoptions, collaborations with U.S. institutions)
- The petitioner has already begun advancing the endeavor in the U.S. (visiting researcher, collaborative grants, conference presentations)

### What to Avoid
- One-paragraph Prong 3 — this signals the attorney didn't engage with the standard
- "It would be beneficial to the U.S." with no specifics — this is the legal standard restated, not an argument
- Repeating Prong 2 credentials here without connecting them to the waiver rationale

### Length
400–600 words. Three well-developed paragraphs minimum.

## Brief Section: Conclusion

### Legal Purpose
The conclusion gives the adjudicator a clean summary and a clear ask. It should not introduce new arguments or new evidence — it summarizes and requests.

### Required Structure
**Paragraph 1 — Three-prong summary**
One sentence per prong, declarative and confident:
- "The petitioner's proposed endeavor of [X] has substantial merit as evidenced by [2–3 specific facts] and is of national importance as demonstrated by its direct alignment with [named federal program]."
- "The petitioner is exceptionally well positioned to advance this endeavor, as shown by [degree], [key metric of achievement — h-index, top grant, key award], and a concrete plan to [specific US activity]."
- "Waiving the labor certification requirement is beneficial to the United States because [impracticality / urgency / unique qualifications — pick the strongest argument]."

**Paragraph 2 — Cite Dhanasar and request approval**
"For the foregoing reasons, the petitioner respectfully requests that USCIS approve this Form I-140 petition for an EB-2 National Interest Waiver pursuant to Matter of Dhanasar, 26 I&N Dec. 884 (AAO 2016)."

**Paragraph 3 — Offer to provide additional evidence (optional but professional)**
"Should the Service require additional evidence in support of any element of this petition, the petitioner respectfully requests the opportunity to supplement the record."

### Tone
Formal. Confident. No hedging ("we hope," "we believe," "it is our view that"). Use "respectfully" once — it is the standard legal term of address. Do not overuse it.

### What to Avoid
- Summarizing more than 3 sentences per prong (the adjudicator read the whole brief)
- New facts not previously established
- Excessive length — 200–300 words maximum
- Ending on a weak note ("we look forward to your favorable decision") instead of a direct request

### Length
200–300 words. Short and definitive.
